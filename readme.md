The Hercules Challenge is a co-design consortium challenge available to first-year (first semester) engineers through the mandatory introductory class, "Introduction to Professional Engineering." The idea behind the Hercules Challenge is to introduce humanitarian engineering to students who might otherwise never consider it.

## IsoPod Background

![MIT License](https://img.shields.io/badge/License-MIT-F3F4F4?style=flat-square&logoColor=black)
![EM](https://img.shields.io/badge/Physics-Electromagnetics-2C2C2C?style=flat-square&logo=physics&logoColor=87CEEB)

The `IsoPod` is a pill system built around an axial shake generator, meaning it relies on no external power. It has multi-compartment storage bays for pills. It does have a Li-Po battery to store power generated by the user's natural motion while moving or shaking on purpose. This could allow it to use an e-ink display to show, via picture graphs, when each storage bay should be taken. It could also have a ring LED that alerts the user during the first minute of each notification.

In humanitarian engineering, systems often help compensate for deficits. `IsoPod` is another example of a device that does this. It addresses medication adherence by leveraging memory and consistency, two areas where machines excel. At the same time, it offers a solution for users in power-constrained environments.

The machine uses `Faraday's Law of Induction`, which states that the rate of change of flux linkage over time produces an `opposing voltage` to the direction of motion. Using related rates from calculus II, we can see the two main components:

$$V_{\text{induced}} = -\frac{d\lambda}{dt} \implies -\frac{d\lambda}{dz} \cdot \frac{dz}{dt}$$

This implies there are two different rates we can manipulate to induce a voltage. Firstly, increasing the rate at which flux linkage over the z-axis changes, or secondly, increasing the rate at which the armature is moving along the z-axis. Both of these can be analytically approximated as:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = A \omega \cos(\omega t + \phi)$$

$$\frac{d\lambda}{dz} = \frac{d}{dz} (\mu H \cos(z)) = -\mu H \sin(z)$$

> [!note]
> Flux linkage can be approximated analytically for intuition, while the final flux linkage is obtained by FEM.

So from these two rates, the three main variables are exposed: increasing the acceleration `A` and frequency `ω` will increase induced voltage and hence energy production, but these are constrained by the user's physical abilities. Whereas the permeability `μ` and `H` will increase the induced voltage while generally not being constrained by the user's physical abilities.

```
TOP (N) (3mm)

↓ (Gap 30mm)

core (N-S|S-N|N-S|S-N|N-S|S-N|N-S|S-N) (80mm) 

↓ (Gap 30mm)

BOTTOM (N) (3mm)
```

Hence the topology above was chosen. The `TOP` and `BOTTOM` magnets create a restoring magnetic force, allowing the device to build velocity from oscillating the device with the user's forearm muscles. The `N-S|S-N` arrangement leads to spikes in the B field over the z-axis, hence increasing induced voltage and therefore power output.

The model was constructed under axial symmetry (Z-R) due to the generator being symmetric around its axis. A Dirichlet boundary condition was used, with the radial boundary distance being `2` x `slot_outer_radius` and the axial boundary distance being `1.2` x `stator_tube_length`. The field condition was asymptotic due to the `FEMMMagnetostaticSolver` magnet source limitation within AC simulations. The environment material was `air` while assuming zero conductivity through the media.

The simulated generator produced a peak voltage of `8.05 V` and an RMS voltage of `4.1 V`, while the internal resistance was `31.7 Ω` with `274 turns` per coil. The power generated was `0.53 W` at `8 Hz` with `20 mm` of stator displacement and a peak-to-peak displacement of `40 mm`.

# Construction

The generator was printed out of ABS on a Voron 2.4. The armature consisted of a carbon fibre rod with `6` x `N52` magnets inserted into it in the pattern described above. The stator consisted of `4` coils with `180` turns per coil, with each coil wound in the opposite direction to the last. The coils were secured in place with `10mm` and `5mm` thick Kapton tape.

The restoring magnets were flat `12mm x 3mm` neodymium magnets. The generator stator was split into two pieces: the main piece, which housed the coils and one restoring magnet, and the cap, which held the other restoring magnet and was attached via `3` x `M3 x 20mm` bolts.

# Results

<div align="center">
  <img src="deliverable_submission\Images\trace.png" alt="Generator trace @ 8.81 Hz" style="max-width:600px;">
  <p><i>Figure 5: Voltage vs time trace @ 10V per vertical division and 50ms per horizontal division</i></p>
</div>

When shaken at 8.81 Hz, the generator produced a peak voltage of `16.8 V` and a negative peak of `-16.4 V`, resulting in a peak-to-peak voltage of `33.2 V`. However, due to the low frequency, the RMS voltage was only `3.47 V`. The generator had an internal resistance of `27.8 Ω`, and using:

$$P_{avg} = \frac{V_{rms}^2}{R}$$

the generator produces `0.43 W` at `8.81 Hz`. This result is interesting because the physical generator had fewer turns per slot than the simulated version, while all other geometric parameters remained the same. This most likely means that the simulation was either underestimating the total change in flux linkage between the stator and armature while overestimating the average change in flux linkage, or that the motion of the stator differed due to the non-linear response of the restoring magnets compared to a clean sinusoidal wave.

Another notable observation is that the trace appears asymmetrical, with a smooth rise but a noisy fall. This can most likely be attributed to degradation of the prototype due to its usage as a demo item.
